WLED Strip Planner

WLED is free, open-source firmware that turns a cheap ESP32 or ESP8266 Wi-Fi board into a controller for addressable LED strips, with a browser interface, an effects library, and hooks for home automation and light shows. The project's own docs cover getting it running. A lot of first-project questions turn out to be about power and wiring, so this page starts there: describe your strip and it does the arithmetic.

How it works it out

J1 Wiring for your strip

Power (+)Ground, shared by everythingData, one way only
  1. One ground for all. The supply, the board, and every feed point share ground. Data is measured against ground, so a board on a separate supply with no common ground gives garbage colours or nothing.
  2. Data has a direction. Strips have arrows printed on them. The controller connects to the input end; feeding the wrong end does nothing.
  3. Lift the data line. ESP boards talk at 3.3 V and 5 V pixels expect close to 5 V. It often works anyway, until it doesn't. A 74AHCT125 or 74HCT125 level shifter between board and strip is the usual fix for a first pixel that flickers or a strip that ignores you. WLED's own wiring guide covers it.
  4. A resistor and a capacitor. A small resistor (a few hundred ohms) in the data line near the first pixel damps ringing, and a large capacitor across the supply at the strip's start soaks up the surge at switch-on. Builders differ on values; both are cheap insurance, not magic.
  5. Feed power, not data, along the way. The thin copper on a strip drops voltage over distance, so the far end goes dim and orange. Extra feeds (power injection) connect + and ground at intervals; the data line stays single.
  6. Fuse each feed. Thin wire carrying many amps from a big supply is how strips start melting. A fuse sized to the wire on each feed protects it.
  7. Don't power a strip through the board. A board's 5 V pin passes current through its USB port and small parts. If you must, keep WLED's limit under about 1 A.

R1 WLED, the software

  1. Pick a board. Both ESP8266 and ESP32 boards run WLED. The ESP32 is the safer pick for a new build: more memory, more LED outputs, more headroom for add-ons.
  2. Flash it. WLED's project page links a web installer that flashes the board over USB from a browser that supports Web Serial, with no toolchain to set up.
  3. Join it to Wi-Fi. A fresh install starts its own access point. Connect to it, give it your network's details, and it reboots onto your Wi-Fi.
  4. Tell it about the strip. In the LED settings: the data pin, the LED type, the pixel count, and the brightness limiter's two numbers from the planner above (supply current and mA per pixel).
  5. Play. Pick an effect and a palette from a phone or browser. Save what you like as a preset.
SSegment

A slice of the strip that runs its own effect and colours. One strip under a shelf can be three segments doing three things.

EEffect

The motion: a pattern over time, like a chase, twinkle, fire, or rainbow. WLED ships with well over a hundred.

PPalette

The colours an effect draws from. The same effect looks completely different in "ocean" and "lava". Effect and palette are picked separately.

PRPreset

A saved snapshot of the whole state: segments, effects, palettes, brightness. Presets are what buttons, schedules, and automations recall.

PLPlaylist

A preset that steps through other presets on a timer, for a display that changes through the evening.

ABLBrightness limiter

WLED estimates current from the pixel count, the mA-per-pixel setting, and the colours it is about to show, and dims the whole strip if the estimate passes the limit you set. It is an estimate, not a measurement, so it protects a supply only if its numbers are honest.

UIWeb and app

Every WLED board serves its own control page on your network; there are phone apps that find boards automatically.

SYNCBoard to board

Boards can broadcast their state over UDP, so a change on one is copied by the others: one look across several rooms.

HAHome automation

Home Assistant has an integration for it, and WLED also speaks MQTT and a JSON API, so lights can follow sensors, schedules, and scenes.

DMXLight-show protocols

E1.31, Art-Net, and DDP let sequencing software stream every pixel's colour in real time, which is how synchronized holiday displays drive many boards at once.

TP Sources and limits

  • WLED knowledge base: the project's own documentation, including LED settings and wiring guides for level shifters.
  • WLED on GitHub: source code and the feature list (segments, presets, sync, and the streaming protocols).
  • The per-pixel figures in the planner are common ratings, not measurements. Strips from different makers draw differently, and WS2815 figures in particular vary from one datasheet and seller to the next. Use your seller's watts-per-metre or a meter at full white if you have them.
  • The feed spacing defaults (about 2 m on 5 V, 5 m on 12 V) are starting rules of thumb. Check the far end at full white: if it shifts toward orange or pink, add a feed.
  • Wire drop uses copper's resistance per metre at room temperature for each gauge, out and back. It does not account for connectors, which often add more drop than the wire.
