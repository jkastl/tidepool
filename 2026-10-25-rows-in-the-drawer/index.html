Rows in the Drawer

SQL on a made-up pathology lab, one clause at a time

Lab data comes nested: a case holds parts, a part holds blocks, a block gives slides, and a slide can be scanned more than once. A lot of SQL mistakes happen right at those joins. This page runs six small queries over a tiny made-up lab, one clause at a time, so you can watch the rows (drawn as index cards) get copied, crossed out, piled up, and numbered. Each query comes in two versions that differ by a keyword or a line. Version A is the one often written first; version B is the fix (or, in the last query, the other way to count).

The drawers

Three tables, small enough to read card by card. Open a drawer to see what is in it.

A slide label like A1-2 on case S26-101 reads: part A, block 1, slide 2 cut from that block. A real lab system usually keeps parts and blocks as their own records; here they are folded into the label to keep the drawers small. Labs format their labels in their own ways.

A few words used below: NULL means no value recorded. A CTE (the WITH … AS part) is a named sub-query written first. LAG reads a value from the previous row. H&E and IHC are kinds of stain; QC is quality control.

Three details are planted on purpose, because real data has them: slide 1 and slide 5 were rescanned, after a blurred scan and a scan that cut off part of the tissue; scan 7's barcode did not read, so it has no slide_id; and slide 7 has not been scanned yet, while slide 8's scan is still waiting on QC.

Six queries, clause by clause

A database reads a query in a different order than it is written. FROM and the joins come first, then WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING (none of the six needs it), window functions, SELECT, and ORDER BY last. Window functions are really worked out as part of SELECT, after HAVING; this page gives them their own step so you can watch them. Pick a query, pick a version, and step through. The highlighted part of the SQL is the clause doing the work.

    What the six show
    
  
      
    
    • The join that doubled. Joining a parent to two child tables copies the parent once per child row. Counting after that counts copies. COUNT(DISTINCT …) is one fix; counting each child table in its own subquery before joining is another.
      • 
    
    • The LEFT JOIN that went missing. A LEFT JOIN keeps unmatched rows with NULLs, and a WHERE test such as = on the right-hand table then throws them away, because NULL = 'pass' is not TRUE. Moving the test into ON keeps them.
      • 
    
    • NOT IN and the unread barcode. x NOT IN (…) is never TRUE if the list holds a NULL, so the query quietly returns nothing. NOT EXISTS does not have this problem.
      • 
    
    • The latest rescan. Window functions are worked out after WHERE, so they cannot be filtered there. Compute the row number in a CTE or subquery and filter outside it.
      • 
    
    • The previous scan. Leaving out PARTITION BY makes LAG reach back across scanners, so a scan that follows the other scanner's work borrows its time (scans 4, 7 and 10 here).
      • 
    
    • Piles or notes in the margin. GROUP BY turns each group into one row. A window function writes the group's total on every row and keeps them all.
