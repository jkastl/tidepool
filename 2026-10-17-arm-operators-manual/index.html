1.Links and joints

An arm is a chain. The rigid pieces are links; what connects them are joints. The chain starts at a fixed base and ends at the end effector, the hand or tool that does the work.

Nearly every joint is one of two kinds. A revolute joint turns, like an elbow. A prismatic joint slides, like a drawer. Each lets the next link move in exactly one way.

FIG. 1 The two basic joints. Each adds one way to move.

2.Degrees of freedom

Each independent joint adds one degree of freedom (DOF). Count them and you know how many ways the arm can move.

Why do so many factory arms have six? A rigid object floating in space has six things you can set: where it is (left–right, forward–back, up–down) and which way it faces (roll, pitch, yaw). To put a tool anywhere in its reach and point it any way, an arm needs at least six joints. Fewer, and some poses are impossible. More (seven is common on collaborative arms) and the arm has spare motion, so it can reach the same pose while swinging its elbow around an obstacle.

FIG. 2 A typical six-axis arm. The first three joints mostly decide where the wrist is; the last three mostly decide which way the tool points.

The figures that follow flatten the arm onto a page: two or three revolute joints in a plane. Every idea carries up to six joints in three dimensions; only the arithmetic gets longer.

3.Forward kinematics

Kinematics is the geometry of motion, ignoring the forces behind it. The easy direction is forward: given every joint angle, where does the hand end up? Add up the links, each turned by the angles before it.

4.Inverse kinematics

The useful direction is backwards. A task says "pick up the part there"; the controller has to work out which joint angles get the hand to that spot. That is inverse kinematics (IK), and it is where arms get interesting:

  • Two answers. A two-link arm can reach most points elbow-up or elbow-down.
  • No answer. Outside the workspace, the ring the arm can reach, there is none. With unequal links there is a hole in the middle, too.
  • Endless answers. Add a joint the task does not need and a whole family of poses reaches the same point. Fix the hand's angle as well and you are back to two.

For two links, IK has a tidy closed form. The law of cosines gives the elbow: cos θ2 = (x² + y² − L1² − L2²) / (2·L1·L2). Taking the positive or negative angle picks elbow-down or elbow-up. If that cosine comes out above 1 or below −1, the point is out of reach. Six-axis arms built with three wrist axes meeting at one point split the same way into a "where" problem and a "which way" problem, each with a closed form; arms without that layout are usually solved numerically, nudging the angles until the hand lands.

Try three joints with the wrist on. The faint arms all put the hand on the target; only the hand angle slider picks one. That spare freedom is called redundancy.

5.Singularities

At certain poses the arm loses a direction it can move in. Stretch a two-link arm straight out: the hand can swing sideways but cannot move further out, however the joints turn. That pose is a singularity.

The other classic one: fold the arm back so the hand sits right over the shoulder joint. Now the shoulder angle no longer matters, since turning it just spins the hand in place, so the arm has lost a direction again.

The trouble shows up near them. Ask the hand to move in a straight line at a steady speed past a singularity, and the joints must spin faster and faster to keep up. Real controllers slow down, stop, or plan around them.

6.Motors, gears, sensors

Every joint has the same small kit: something to turn it, something to multiply the force, something to measure where it is, and a controller closing the loop between them, thousands of times a second.

Table 1: joint hardware
PartCommon choicesWhat it trades
MotorBrushless servo motor; stepper motor; hobby servoBrushless servos are smooth and strong but need a proper drive. Steppers are cheap and hold position without sensors until overloaded, then silently lose steps. Hobby servos bundle motor, gears, and sensor for small arms.
Reducer (gearing)Strain wave ("harmonic") gear; cycloidal drive; planetary gearbox; beltsMotors spin fast and weak; joints need slow and strong. Strain wave gears give high ratios with almost no backlash in a compact package, which is why wrists use them. Cycloidal drives take shock loads in big joints. Planetary gears are cheaper but have more play.
SensorOptical or magnetic encoder, incremental or absoluteAn incremental encoder counts ticks from a known home; an absolute one knows its angle at power-on, so the arm needs no homing routine.
BrakeSpring-applied brake on each heavy axisHolds the arm when power is cut, so it does not fold under its own weight.
ControllerPer-joint drive plus a motion controllerTurns a planned path into joint commands, checks limits, and compares where each joint is with where it should be.

Specs worth knowing

Reach: how far from the base the wrist can get. Payload: the mass it can carry at full reach, tool included. Repeatability: how closely it returns to the same taught point, often a few hundredths of a millimetre on industrial arms. Accuracy is different: how close it gets to a point given as coordinates, which depends on how well its link lengths and offsets are known. Most arms are far more repeatable than accurate.

7.End effectors

The arm only moves the tool. The tool decides the job, and on many cells a tool changer swaps it automatically.

Many tools add a force-torque sensor at the wrist, so the arm can feel contact: pressing a part home, sanding with steady pressure, or stopping when it bumps something.

8.Arm families

Same parts, different layouts, each shaped by the job.

Articulated

Revolute joints all the way, usually six. Reaches around things; the general-purpose shape for welding, painting, handling.

FIG. 6

SCARA

Two horizontal revolute joints plus a vertical slide. Stiff up and down, flexible side to side: fast, precise assembly on a flat table. The name means Selective Compliance Assembly Robot Arm.

FIG. 7

Delta

Three light arms from an overhead base meet at one small platform. Very fast pick-and-place, small payloads; the classic packaging-line robot.

FIG. 8

Cartesian (gantry)

Three prismatic joints at right angles: x, y, z. Simple maths, easy to scale up, the same layout as most 3D printers and CNC routers.

FIG. 9

Collaborative

Usually articulated, built to work beside people: rounded, lighter, and limited in force and speed so a bump stops it. Often taught by hand-guiding the arm through a move.

9.Glossary

Actuator
Whatever moves a joint: usually an electric motor, sometimes hydraulics or pneumatics.
Backlash
Play in the gears: the little bit a joint can move before the gears engage.
Degree of freedom
One independent way to move. One per joint, in a simple chain.
End effector
The tool at the end of the arm.
Encoder
A sensor that reports a joint's angle.
Forward kinematics
Joint angles in, hand position out. Always one answer.
Inverse kinematics
Hand position in, joint angles out. None, a few, or infinitely many answers.
Link
A rigid piece between two joints.
Payload
The mass an arm can carry at full reach, tool included.
Pose
A position plus an orientation.
Prismatic joint
A joint that slides.
Redundancy
More joints than the task needs, so many poses reach the same goal.
Repeatability
How closely an arm returns to the same taught point.
Revolute joint
A joint that turns.
Singularity
A pose where the arm loses a direction of motion; near one, small hand motions need huge joint motions.
Teach pendant
The handheld control box used to jog an arm and record points.
Workspace
Every point the hand can reach.
